\title{What Survives a Strict Split? An Empirical Audit of\\Long-Document Screenplay Modelling for IMDb Rating Prediction}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous authors}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Screenplay-to-rating regression is usually reported on a random split of an archival corpus. We argue that this number is not the number a reader wants, and we measure the gap. Working from a corpus of $5{,}204$ IMSDb screenplays joined to IMDb metadata, we first run a record-identity audit --- content SHA-256 digests, IMDb identifiers, title$\times$year keys, and a normalised title-family key --- and publish the resulting exclusion ledger, retaining $5{,}164$ records after removing nine sub-1\,KB fragments, four corrupt release years, and twenty-seven records whose positional identity in the archived embedding matrix cannot be resolved uniquely. We then quantify a defect invisible at the API surface: the pipeline slides a $256$-word window over each script, but the encoder admits only $254$ WordPiece tokens, so $98.45\%$ of windows are cut and $31.60\%$ of all tokens never reach the model. Finally, we evaluate identical representations and learners under three partitions: random $k$-fold, title-family \textsc{GroupKFold}, and a chronological 2020--2025 holdout. Skill is strongly protocol-dependent: the best system reports $R^2=0.549$ $[0.530, 0.567]$ under random folds, $0.536$ $[0.516, 0.554]$ once franchises are held together, and $0.309$ $[0.199, 0.404]$ when the test set lies in the future. Block-permutation analysis on the temporal holdout explains why. Release year and decade, which jointly supply $36.6\%$ of in-sample split gain, contribute exactly $\Delta\text{MAE}=0.000$ once test years fall outside the training range, while runtime alone ($+0.199$) matches the entire $384$-dimensional sentence-embedding block ($+0.195$). Semantic embeddings survive the strict split; the calendar confound does not.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
data leakage, evaluation protocol, sentence embeddings, long-document NLP, screenplay analysis, temporal generalisation, grouped cross-validation.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

A feature-film screenplay runs to roughly $25{,}000$ words. Sentence encoders read $256$ tokens. That mismatch is the central engineering problem in screenplay modelling, and the usual answer --- slide a window, embed each window, average the results --- is cheap, reproducible, and widely used. What is far less often examined is whether the evaluation wrapped around that pipeline measures anything a practitioner could rely on, because an archival screenplay corpus assembled from a community repository carries two structural hazards that a shuffled train/test split will silently convert into apparent skill.

The first hazard is duplication. Community archives accumulate the same work under several file names, franchises share vocabulary and structure across installments, and remakes reuse plot and dialogue wholesale. Under a random partition, a near-copy in the training set makes its twin in the test set trivially predictable. The effect is not exotic; it is the dominant failure mode catalogued in surveys of leakage-driven irreproducibility across applied machine learning~\cite{kapoor2023}, and has been documented specifically for train--test overlap in NLP benchmarks~\cite{lewis2021,elangovan2021}.

The second hazard is time. Ratings in an archival corpus are collected once, at scrape time, but the films span a century, and both the selection process and the rating behaviour of the audience drift. A model given a release-year feature can recover a large share of target variance from the calendar alone, without reading a single line of dialogue. That shortcut evaporates the moment the test films are drawn from years the model never saw, which is the only setting in which a screenplay-stage predictor would actually be deployed.

Layered underneath both is a quieter defect specific to chunked long-document pipelines. Chunking is almost always specified in words; truncation always happens in subword tokens. Nobody is told when the budget is exceeded --- the encoder simply drops the tail --- so a configuration that reads as ``$256$-word windows with $50$-word overlap'' can in practice discard a third of the corpus without emitting a single warning.

This paper is an audit, not a leaderboard entry. We take an existing SBERT-plus-gradient-boosting pipeline over $5{,}204$ IMSDb screenplays and ask what its reported performance is actually made of. Three contributions follow:
\begin{itemize}
\item \hlt{C1 --- Record-identity protocol and exclusion ledger.} We define film identity through four keys --- content SHA-256 digest, IMDb identifier, title$\times$year, and a normalised title-family stem --- audit all $5{,}204$ joined records against each, and report every exclusion (Section~\ref{sec:corpus}). The audited corpus contains no duplicate identifiers and no duplicate title$\times$year pairs, but 80 title collisions and 164 multi-member title families that a random split would straddle.
\item \hlt{C2 --- Measurement of silent truncation.} Running the encoder's own tokeniser over the pipeline's own windows, we find that $98.45\%$ of $256$-word windows exceed the $254$-token content budget and that $31.60\%$ of WordPiece tokens are discarded before the encoder sees them, with a median window of 371 tokens against a budget of 254 (Section~\ref{sec:tokens}).
\item \hlt{C3 --- Three-protocol evaluation with paired inference.} Holding representation and learner fixed, we vary only the partition: random $k$-fold, title-family \textsc{GroupKFold}, and a chronological 2020--2025 holdout. We report bootstrap intervals, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with matched-pairs effect sizes, and a block-permutation attribution that isolates which feature families survive temporal extrapolation (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:xai}).
\end{itemize}

We do not claim a new state of the art. We claim that the difference between $R^2=0.549$ and $R^2=0.309$ on the same corpus, the same features, and the same learner is the finding.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Script-driven forecasting and narrative analysis.} Early work extracted features from scripts to forecast commercial outcomes: Eliashberg et al.~\cite{ref3} used spec-script descriptors in kernel regression to predict ROI, while Hunter et al.~\cite{ref4} targeted box-office earnings. Parallel research maps narrative structures, such as emotional arc typologies~\cite{ref5}, character dialogue linguistics~\cite{ref6}, and multimodal channels~\cite{ref12}. Downstream script tasks include MPAA age-suitability ratings~\cite{ref10,ref34}, severity classification~\cite{ref11}, and tag prediction~\cite{ref35}. Screenplay quality prediction in awards settings~\cite{ref38} shares our fundamental obstacle: ratings represent social aggregations only partially dictated by textual content.

\textbf{Metadata and multimodal forecasting.} Alternative forecasting leverages non-textual channels: production budgets and cast metadata~\cite{ref15}, social media volume~\cite{ref21,ref23}, Twitter chatter~\cite{ref22}, Wikipedia page views~\cite{ref24}, trailers~\cite{ref16}, and review sentiment~\cite{ref18,ref28}. Tabular IMDb rating regression is widely studied~\cite{ref17,ref26,ref29}, including deep models for serials~\cite{ref27} and joint sentiment-diffusion networks~\cite{ref30}. Vogel~\cite{ref25} notes the natural ceiling: box-office and audience reception are heavily governed by release timing, competition, and distribution budgets unobservable in a script.

\textbf{Long-document representations.} Sentence-BERT~\cite{ref2} yields dense sentence representations from Siamese objectives. Applying fixed-length encoders to book- or script-length documents requires hierarchical attention~\cite{ref19}, sparse attention transformers~\cite{ref1}, or chunk-and-pool averaging. Chunk-and-pool is the prevailing default because it avoids expensive fine-tuning, runs on CPU, and scales linearly. Classical $n$-gram TF-IDF remains a strong lexical baseline on long technical text.

\textbf{Data leakage and evaluation protocol design.} Gorman and Bedrick~\cite{gorman2019} proved that POS tagger rankings permute under alternative splits, prompting S{\o}gaard et al.~\cite{sogaard2021} to urge split designs aligned with claimed generalization. Lewis et al.~\cite{lewis2021} and Elangovan et al.~\cite{elangovan2021} formalized train--test leakage and overlap measurement in NLP benchmarks. Kapoor and Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023} catalogued leakage failure modes across applied ML, arguing for pre-registered partitions. Temporal out-of-distribution evaluation~\cite{lazaridou2021} and corpus provenance documentation~\cite{bender2018,dodge2021} further highlight the necessity of strict split designs.

\section{Corpus Integrity and Exclusion Audit}
\label{sec:corpus}

\subsection{Source and Join}
The corpus joins full screenplay texts scraped from the Internet Movie Script Database (IMSDb) to IMDb metadata. Each of the $5{,}204$ joined records contains title, release year, decade bin, IMDb identifier (\texttt{tt} ID), runtime in minutes, IMDb rating ($1.0$--$10.0$ scale, one-decimal resolution), and script text path. Ratings exhibit mean $5.98$, median $5.80$, standard deviation $1.44$; runtimes span $45$--$254$ minutes (median 99).

\subsection{Four Identity Keys}
Deduplication requires explicit identity criteria. We audit four keys of decreasing strictness:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Content digest:} $\text{SHA-256}(b)$ of raw bytes $b$. Collisions resolve by preserving the earliest record in load order.
\item \emph{IMDb identifier:} Canonical \texttt{tt}-identifier catches identical films scraped under varied titles.
\item \emph{Title$\times$year:} Fallback for missing or malformed IDs.
\item \emph{Title family:} Normalizes title $t$ by lowercasing, stripping non-alphanumeric tokens and leading articles, and iteratively stripping trailing sequel markers (Arabic/Roman numerals, ``Part $N$''). Families group installments together (e.g., \emph{Toy Story 1--3} map to \texttt{toy story}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Exclusion Flow and Audit Results}
Let $\mathcal{R}_0$ be the initial set ($|\mathcal{R}_0|=5{,}204$). Four nested filters apply sequentially: $\mathcal{R}_k = \{r \in \mathcal{R}_{k-1} : \pi_k(r)\}$ where $\pi_{\text{len}}$ requires $|text| \ge 1{,}000$ chars; $\pi_{\text{hash}}$ checks byte uniqueness; $\pi_{\text{year}}$ enforces release year $\in [1900, 2025]$; and $\pi_{\text{id}}$ guarantees unique positional mapping in the archived embedding matrix:
\begin{equation}
5{,}204 \xrightarrow{\pi_{\text{len}}} 5{,}195 \xrightarrow{\pi_{\text{hash}}} 5{,}195 \xrightarrow{\pi_{\text{year}}} 5{,}191 \xrightarrow{\pi_{\text{id}}} 5{,}164
\end{equation}

\section{Methodology and Representations}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Screenplay Feature Extraction}
Nineteen numeric predictors are extracted: 16 structural statistics from raw scripts plus 3 metadata fields (runtime, release year, decade). The 16 script features encompass size measures (\texttt{char\_count}, \texttt{word\_count}, \texttt{line\_count}, \texttt{sentence\_count}, \texttt{scene\_count}, \texttt{words\_per\_scene}), lexical sophistication (\texttt{avg\_word\_length}, \texttt{unique\_word\_ratio}, \texttt{long\_word\_ratio}), sentence geometry (\texttt{avg\_sentence\_length}, \texttt{sentence\_length\_std}), and dialogue/affect proxies (\texttt{dialogue\_density}, \texttt{unique\_characters}, \texttt{exclamation\_ratio}, \texttt{question\_ratio}, \texttt{action\_density}).

\emph{Degeneracy audit:} Mean \texttt{dialogue\_density} is $0.0087$ ($\pm 0.0361$) and mean \texttt{unique\_characters} is $3.41$. The extraction regular expression assumed a strict \texttt{NAME:} cue absent from most IMSDb formats, rendering both features transcription artifacts rather than narrative characteristics.

\subsection{Token-Aligned Chunking and Silent Truncation}
\label{sec:tokens}
Given word sequence $D = (w_1, \ldots, w_N)$, windows of length $L=256$ words with overlap $o=50$ yield stride $s = L - o = 206$. Window $j$ is $C_j = (w_{(j-1)s+1}, \ldots, w_{\min((j-1)s+L, N)})$, producing $M = \lceil (N-L)/s \rceil + 1$ windows. Each window is encoded via $f: C \to \mathbb{R}^d$ ($d=384$, \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}), and document pooling is the arithmetic mean:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}(D) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^M f(C_j)
\end{equation}

Because $f(C_j)$ is $L_2$-normalised, the squared norm of the pooled vector is:
\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{e}(D)\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{M^2}\left(M + 2\sum_{1 \le i < j \le M} \langle f(C_i), f(C_j) \rangle\right)
\end{equation}
which falls toward $1/M$ as window embeddings decorrelate. With a median of $M=36$ windows per script, localized scene signal is aggressively diluted.

\textbf{Silent token truncation:} The transformer accepts at most $T_{\max}=256$ positions, 2 reserved for \texttt{[CLS]} and \texttt{[SEP]}, leaving a content budget $B = 254$ WordPiece tokens. Letting $\tau(\cdot)$ be the tokenizer, the encoder observes $\tau(C_j)$ truncated to $B$. The discarded token fraction for document $D$ is:
\begin{equation}
\rho(D) = 1 - \frac{\sum_{j=1}^M \min(|\tau(C_j)|, B)}{\sum_{j=1}^M |\tau(C_j)|}
\end{equation}

Evaluating $\tau$ across 14,558 windows from 356 scripts reveals that 256 English words average $369.3$ WordPiece tokens (median 371, 99th percentile 453, maximum 613). Consequently, \textbf{98.45\% of windows exceed the token limit}, and \textbf{31.60\% of all generated tokens are silently dropped} before encoding.

\subsection{Models and Partition Protocols}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Lexical Control:} TF-IDF on unigrams/bigrams (8,000 features, English stop words removed, min df 3, max df 0.85, sublinear tf) with gradient-boosted trees.
\item \textbf{Learners:} Gradient-boosted decision trees (500 estimators, lr 0.05, max depth 6, early stopping 20 rounds, clipped $[1.0, 10.0]$). Reference probes include OLS on metadata and Ridge ($\alpha=1.5$) on standardized embeddings.
\item \textbf{Protocols:} We evaluate three split designs:
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{P1 (Random):} Shuffled 5-fold CV (average franchise overlap $\lambda = 5.50\%$).
  \item \textbf{P2 (Grouped):} \textsc{GroupKFold} by title family ($|G|=4{,}979$, $\lambda = 0\%$). Fold sizes ($1{,}032$--$1{,}033$) match P1.
  \item \textbf{P3 (Chronological):} Train on release years $\le 2015$ ($n=3{,}918$); validate on 2016--2019 ($n=731$); test on 2020--2025 ($n=515$, $\lambda = 6.41\%$).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical Inference}
Estimates carry 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals ($B=1{,}000$). System differences are tested via the two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on absolute errors $e_i^A - e_i^B$, reporting non-tied pairs, statistic $W$, exact $p$-value, and matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation $r = (R^+ - R^-)/(R^+ + R^-) \in [-1, 1]$.

\subsection{Lexical Control and Ensembling}
On the 5-fold CV subset with full raw text ($n=5{,}195$), TF-IDF+XGBoost ($R^2 = 0.433 \pm 0.014$) performs on par with 16 structural statistics ($R^2 = 0.440 \pm 0.007$). SBERT outperforms TF-IDF by $\Delta\text{MAE} = 0.0514$ ($p = 2.6 \times 10^{-5}$). Ensembling all representations via Ridge stacking yields $R^2 = 0.577$, and TPE hyperparameter tuning reaches $R^2 = 0.581$. These model optimization gains ($+0.032$) are minor compared to the $0.240$ $R^2$ lost to the temporal shift.

\section{Feature Attribution and Mechanism Analysis}
\label{sec:xai}

\subsection{Split Gain vs. Deployment Utility}
In the gradient-boosted model, 384 SBERT dimensions account for 55.45\% of total split gain (mean 0.14\% per dimension), 3 metadata columns account for 36.57\% (\texttt{movie\_length} 25.50\%, \texttt{year} 11.01\%, \texttt{decade} 0.06\%), and 16 structural statistics account for 7.98\% (dominated by length proxies \texttt{char\_count} 2.41\% and \texttt{word\_count} 1.56\%).

\subsection{Block Permutation on the Temporal Holdout}
Permuting feature blocks across the 515 holdout films (20 iterations; baseline $\text{MAE} = 0.7232$) reveals the mechanism of temporal breakdown:
\begin{itemize}
\item Permuting all metadata: $\Delta\text{MAE} = \mathbf{+0.2168 \pm 0.0250}$
\item Permuting \texttt{movie\_length} alone: $\Delta\text{MAE} = \mathbf{+0.1988 \pm 0.0185}$
\item Permuting 384 SBERT embeddings: $\Delta\text{MAE} = \mathbf{+0.1950 \pm 0.0217}$
\item Permuting \texttt{year}: $\Delta\text{MAE} = \mathbf{+0.0000 \pm 0.0000}$
\item Permuting \texttt{decade\_encoded}: $\Delta\text{MAE} = \mathbf{+0.0000 \pm 0.0000}$
\end{itemize}

The exact zero increase for calendar features is structural: because all test films postdate the training range ($>2015$), test instances traverse the identical tree branches regardless of release year. The feature supplying 11\% of in-sample gain becomes entirely inert at deployment. In contrast, runtime (\texttt{movie\_length}) transfers cleanly across decades, reproducing the predictive contribution of the entire 384-dimensional embedding block.

\subsection{Mechanisms Limiting Semantic Signal}
While SBERT embeddings contribute $+0.1950$ MAE under permutation, gain is distributed diffusely across all dimensions. Arithmetic mean pooling across 36 unit-norm window vectors compresses script embeddings toward the corpus centroid, attenuating localized dialogue cues. Combined with the 31.60\% token loss from silent truncation, the semantic pipeline operates over a heavily smoothed representation.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Subset text audit:} Byte SHA-256 deduplication was verified on 356 distributed scripts (0 duplicates). While metadata keys confirm zero duplicate IDs across all 5,204 records, unobserved text collisions among non-distributed scripts remain possible.
\item \emph{IMSDb curation bias:} Corpus mean ratings fall from 7.87 in the 1920s to 5.48 in the 2010s, reflecting community curation preferences. Models reading \texttt{year} exploit this artifact.
\item \emph{Target variance compression:} Rating standard deviation falls from 1.46 (pre-2020) to 1.17 (2020--2025). Part of the $R^2$ drop is mathematical ($R^2$ scales with target variance), while MAE remains stable ($0.7232$).
\item \emph{Deployment unavailability of runtime:} Runtime drives temporal transfer, yet runtime is unknown prior to film production. Defaulting metadata to constants reduces deployed performance to the text-only baseline ($R^2=0.267$).
\item \emph{Observational ceiling:} Ratings aggregate direction, acting, marketing, and cultural timing. A screenplay model establishes a lower bound on text predictive power, not an upper ceiling on commercial forecasting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

Evaluating screenplay-to-rating predictors under shuffled train/test partitions creates an illusion of predictive skill. On 5,164 audited IMSDb scripts, random 5-fold CV yields $R^2 = 0.549$; grouping by franchise reduces this to $0.536$; and holding out future release years drops performance to $R^2 = 0.309$. Block-permutation analysis confirms that release year contributes 11\% of in-sample split gain but zero utility on future holdouts, while runtime matches the explanatory power of the entire 384-dimensional sentence-embedding block. Furthermore, naive word chunking silently discards 31.60\% of tokens before encoding.

We recommend five operational practices for long-document screenplay modeling:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Group by title family:} Prevent sequel/remake leakage through stem-based grouping.
\item \textbf{Evaluate chronologically:} Use temporal holdouts to reflect actual deployment conditions.
\item \textbf{Report MAE alongside $R^2$:} Disentangle genuine error growth from target variance compression.
\item \textbf{Tokenize before chunking:} Align window budgets to subword tokenizers rather than whitespace word counts.
\item \textbf{Store immutable record keys:} Avoid caching embeddings by row index alone.
\end{enumerate}
